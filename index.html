The problem is not access. It is comparison.

The European Banking Authority now publishes large EU and EEA banks' prudential disclosures — capital, credit risk, liquidity, pay — in one place, as structured XBRL-CSV. That was a genuine step forward, and it solved the wrong half of the problem. The official portal hands you one bank's archive at a time. It will not tell you whether a capital ratio is high, whether a country exposure is unusual, or whether a reported figure is plausible at all.

Those questions need the population as a yardstick. So we assembled it: every filing parsed, every data point resolved against the EBA's own dictionary, every template rebuilt with its real row and column labels — and then, crucially, compared across banks.

The difference shows up fast. One filing in this corpus reports fixed board pay of €11.7 trillion for nine people, roughly three times Germany's GDP. Read on its own it looks like a number. Read against 330 comparable filings, whose median is €1.6 million, it looks like what it is. We do not correct it — we mark it, and we say why.

What this does that the official portal does not

  • Ranks and distributions. Peer groups by size class, consolidation scope and reference date; percentile bands rather than bare league tables. An outlier appears at the edge of a distribution, not at the top of a list.
  • Plausibility against the population. Around 6% of reports carry a value outside the Tukey fences of their own peer group in a given metric. Flagged, never hidden, never altered.
  • Country exposure across banks. Home-market share, a Herfindahl index of country concentration, and an honest quality flag for the residual bucket. The median bank in this corpus keeps 82% of its exposure at home.
  • Time series across reference dates — with the caveat that the taxonomy itself changed between framework versions, and 103 institutions now span that break.

What it is not

It is not supervision, and it is not a scoreboard. Comparability across banks is genuinely limited: accounting standards differ, consolidation scopes differ, national options differ, and the reporting framework changed mid-corpus. Every ranking here carries that caveat, and we would rather say “we cannot tell” than imply a precision the data does not support.

A missing figure is not a zero. Banks may lawfully omit disclosures under CRR Article 432, and a template we cannot place is our gap, not theirs. The viewer distinguishes the two everywhere, because conflating them would quietly turn silence into evidence.
